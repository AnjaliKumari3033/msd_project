Can a Machine Truly Understand Movie Reviews?

We trained and compared ten different machine learning algorithms on 100,000 real IMDB reviews. Our goal: determine whether an algorithm can tell if a person liked a movie, predict their exact score from 1 to 10 stars, and group reviews without any labels.

[01] The Problem

The Problem Statement (PS): What Are We Solving?

When people write movie reviews online, they don't just say “this is good” or “this is bad”. They use sarcasm, tell stories, complain about the director, praise the soundtrack, and write long paragraphs.

Our Core Question

Can a computer automatically read a review written in everyday English and accurately understand how much the viewer liked or disliked the film?

To answer this, we broke the problem down into three distinct tasks:

Task 1

Positive or Negative?

Binary Classification: We give the model a review and ask a simple yes-or-no question: is this review positive or negative? We tested everything from basic word-counting formulas up to modern deep learning models.

Task 2

What is the Exact Star Rating?

Continuous Regression: Instead of just “good” or “bad”, can the model guess the reviewer's exact rating on a scale of 1 to 10 stars based purely on their written words?

Task 3

Can It Group Reviews Without Help?

Unsupervised Clustering: What if we remove all labels and ratings completely? Can the algorithm automatically discover natural groupings and sort reviews into similar topics on its own?

Why is this hard for a computer?

Challenge 01

Negation & Sarcasm

A simple computer model checks for positive words like “good” or negative words like “bad”. But in real speech, someone might say:

  • “This movie was not bad at all.” (Positive meaning, but contains the word “bad”)
  • “A masterpiece of terrible acting.” (Sarcastic insult, but contains the praise word “masterpiece”)
Challenge 02

Mixed Feelings & Review Length

Many reviews are mixed: “The cinematography was breathtaking, but the story was boring and slow.” Humans easily weigh these two opinions, but simpler algorithms struggle to know which emotion mattered more to the reviewer.

[02] The Data

The Dataset: 100,000 Real IMDB Reviews

We used the benchmark IMDB Large Movie Review Dataset, which contains 100,000 real reviews written by real moviegoers. Because our project tackles three different tasks, we split the 100,000 reviews into three purpose-built sets:

Subset Name Number of Reviews What It Contains How We Use It
1. Classification Set 50,000 reviews 25,000 positive and 25,000 negative reviews. Perfectly 50/50 balanced. Used for Task 1 to teach models to classify a review as either Positive or Negative without any unfair bias.
2. Rating Prediction Set 50,000 reviews 50,000 reviews where each file contains the reviewer's exact rating (1 to 10 stars). Used for Task 2 to teach models to predict exact numerical ratings (e.g. predicting a 7.5 out of 10).
3. Unsupervised Set 50,000 reviews 50,000 reviews with all labels, folders, and scores stripped away. Used for Task 3 to test clustering algorithms (K-Means and GMM) on raw text with zero supervision.

Where did the exact 1–10 star ratings come from?

The original dataset had the score hidden inside each file name! For example, a file called 1234_7.txt meant review #1234 was given an exact user score of 7 out of 10. We wrote a Python script using regular expressions to extract that number for every single review.

[03] Preprocessing

How We Cleaned the Data (Data Preprocessing)

Reviews scraped from the web are full of messy website code, weird symbols, and duplicate posts. Before any model could learn from the text, we put all 100,000 reviews through a 4-step cleaning process:

Step 01

Removed Web & HTML Tags

More than 58% of the raw reviews were polluted with <br /> break tags from website code. We stripped all HTML tags out so our models learn real language instead of web markup.

Step 02

Fixed Encoded Symbols

Web scrapers often convert characters into computer codes like &amp; for “&” or &quot; for quote marks. We decoded these back into clean, readable text.

Step 03

Cleaned Messy Spacing

We removed random line breaks, accidental double-spaces, and tabs so every review was formatted as smooth, standard paragraphs.

Step 04

Deleted Duplicate Reviews

We ran a duplicate check and found 96 duplicate reviews in the training set and 199 in the testing set. We deleted them so the models couldn't cheat by memorizing identical reviews across test runs.

Turning Words into Numbers

Computers can only do math on numbers, not letters. To feed reviews into our models, we used two different approaches:

Approach A • For Traditional Models

TF-IDF Word Counting

Counts how often important words and 2-word phrases (like “worst movie” or “must see”) appear in a review, while ignoring generic filler words like “the” and “is”.

Approach B • For Deep Learning & BERT

Token Embeddings & Attention

Breaks words into smaller pieces and keeps the exact order of the sentence intact, allowing the neural network to understand context, grammar, and sentence flow.

[04] Key Discoveries

What Are the Dataset “Anomalies” and Patterns?

When people talk about “dataset anomalies” or patterns, they mean: what weird, surprising, or unexpected habits did we discover about how people write reviews? When auditing our 100,000 reviews, we found 6 fascinating behavioral patterns:

Pattern 01

People Rarely Leave Average Scores (The Missing Middle)

When you look at the 1–10 star ratings, scores 5 and 6 are almost completely missing! Why? First, online reviewers rarely bother writing a review unless they loved a film (10/10) or hated it (1/10). Second, the creators of the IMDB dataset intentionally removed 5 and 6 star reviews to keep positive and negative reviews distinctly separated.

Pattern 02

Angry Reviewers “Shout” with Punctuation (The Anger Index)

When someone loves a movie, they write polite, descriptive adjectives like “brilliant” and “masterpiece”. But when someone hates a movie (1 star), they express anger through punctuation: 1-star reviews had nearly 4 times more exclamation marks (!!!) and ALL-CAPS words than 10-star reviews!

Pattern 03

Strong Opinions Make People Write Longer Reviews

Reviews with 1 star or 10 stars were substantially longer (averaging over 1,420 characters) than mild reviews (under 950 characters). If someone is furious or thrilled, they write a miniature essay explaining why, whereas average opinions get a quick couple of sentences.

Pattern 04

Single Words Are Useless; Word Pairs (Bigrams) Matter

Words like “movie” or “film” appear in almost every single review and give the computer zero clues about sentiment. But looking at 2-word pairs (called bigrams) like “worst movie ever” or “highly recommend” immediately tells the model the sentiment.

Pattern 05

Long Reviews Trip Up Standard Recurrent Networks (LSTM)

Older recurrent neural networks (like standard LSTMs) read reviews word-by-word. When a review is 300 words long, the model tends to forget what was said at the beginning by the time it reaches the end. This is why modern Transformers (like BERT) win: they look at every word simultaneously.

Pattern 06

Mixed Reviews Need “Soft” Probabilities, Not Rigid Boxes

When someone writes “great acting, but a terrible story”, rigid clustering (like K-Means) is forced to make a clumsy guess. Probabilistic models (like Gaussian Mixture Models) work much better by calculating: “this review is 60% positive and 40% negative”.
